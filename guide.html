The pattern guide

No single rotation fits every family.

A practical comparison of the schedules families actually use — how often you exchange, how long each stay lasts, and what to agree on before the first handoff.

Updated 26 October 2026 · About 8 minutes

Every rotation is a trade-off. More exchanges can mean more contact; longer blocks mean fewer transitions but longer stretches apart. The right answer depends on school rhythms, ages, travel distance, work patterns, and how handoffs actually feel — not on a single “correct” pattern.

The one rule that matters most: write down the handoff time and place, then apply it consistently. A simple schedule with reliable handoffs usually beats an elegant schedule that nobody can follow.

How to choose without overthinking it

  1. Start with the school week. Who handles drop-off, pickup, homework and bedtime on school nights? A schedule that fights the school routine will be the hardest to keep.
  2. Count the handoffs, not just the days. Every exchange costs time, planning and emotional energy. Fewer handoffs are easier; longer stretches can be harder for younger children.
  3. Look hard at the weekend. Several 50/50 patterns can give one parent most weekend time. If that is not acceptable, change the exchange day or split the weekend rather than pretending the split is even.
  4. Write the exceptions before you need them. Holidays, school breaks, birthdays, travel and handoff changes cause more conflict than the base rotation. The calendar is only as useful as its rules.
  5. Review after a school term. A schedule that worked in September may not work in January. A planned review is cheaper than an emergency change.

Why the start day matters

All of these patterns repeat from the date you choose. A 2-2-3 that starts on a Monday gives one parent Friday-to-Sunday, then the other parent the following Friday-to-Sunday. Start it on a Tuesday and Friday can belong to one parent while Saturday belongs to the other.

Both Homes includes a “Use that week’s Monday” button for exactly this reason. If your agreement names a different exchange day, keep that day and check the resulting weekend time in the calendar before you commit.

Holidays and school breaks

Most parenting plans separate the everyday rotation from holiday time. That is usually sensible: a holiday rule should override the rotation, not compete with it.

  • Alternate the big ones by year. “Thanksgiving with Parent A in odd years, Parent B in even years” is clearer than splitting the day in half.
  • Define a window, not just a date. “Winter break from the last school bell until 6 p.m. on 1 January” leaves less room for disagreement than “Christmas”.
  • Split long breaks deliberately. Halves, alternating weeks, or a fixed handover date all work. Put the handover time and place in the rule.
  • Decide what happens when a holiday overlaps a swap. The usual convention is that the holiday rule wins; write it down so neither parent has to guess.

The builder supports fixed dates, nth weekdays (for example, the fourth Thursday in November), recurring ranges, alternating years, and splitting a stretch in half. It does not compute Easter or every regional holiday — add those as specific dates if your agreement uses them.

What the overnight numbers mean

Both Homes counts overnights: the parent who has the child overnight gets the night. It does not count daytime hours, school time, or time with grandparents and other caregivers.

That distinction matters. A weekend day is longer than a school day, so 50/50 overnights is not automatically 50/50 hours. Use the overnight count as a planning estimate and a conversation starter, not as a legal timeshare calculation. If a court order uses an hours-based method, follow that method.

The summary covers twelve whole months from the plan’s start month. A leap year includes 366 nights; holiday nights are counted once, in the home that has them.

Sharing a plan without oversharing

A Both Homes share link carries the plan in the URL itself. Nothing is uploaded, but anyone with the link can read what is in it. Names and notes are left out by default; holiday labels remain so the calendar still makes sense.

For a mediator, a school, or a group chat, leave names off and share the exported .ics files or a printed copy instead. Local plans live in one browser on one device, so export a backup before changing devices or clearing site data.

The short version

  • Most contact: 2-2-3, with frequent handoffs.
  • Fewest handoffs: alternating weeks.
  • Weekend in both homes: 2‑2‑3 or 2‑2‑5‑5, started on a Monday.
  • School-week anchor: weekdays / weekends, but it is not 50/50.
  • Always: write the handoff time, place and holiday rules.
